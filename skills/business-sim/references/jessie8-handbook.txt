1 - Overview
1.1 – Simulation principle
Jessie is a business simulation whose objective is to allow participants to progress in the
mastery of marketing and strategic management tools, regardless of their previous level
in the field. This objective was submitted, during the development of the specifications of
the simulation and their computerized application, to the search for a compromise
between simplicity (therefore playability) and complexity (therefore interest).
Simplicity is present in simulation for the following reasons:
• The decisions to be made are few and far between (about twenty per period of play)
• A number of indications and automatic corrections naturally lead teams to
reasonable options
• In case of lack of imagination or self-confidence, participants can still make decisions
mainly based on mimicry (imitation of the choice of the right teams)
Complexity is present in simulation for the following reasons:
• The set of parameters and functions taken into account in the simulation are much
more important than the small number of decisions to be made might lead one to
believe
• The effects of the different decisions taken are interdependent (complexity is
therefore not additive, but multiplicative)
• The results include a significant amount of dependence on the decisions of other
teams
Jessie's simplicity allows everyone to participate, but its complexity makes the system
impossible to control perfectly, even by an expert. In particular, there are no real "magic
formulas" or "miracle recipes" (in technical terms, we speak of convergence zones) which,
once discovered, would empty the simulation of its interest, and it is in the long term that
it gives its full measure.
1.2 – Management disciplines covered
Although Jessie is primarily a simulation focused on marketing strategy, almost all
management disciplines are attached to it, with the exception of tax system analysis:
• finance through a large mechanism for the circulation of funds, which are the subject
of a permanent arbitration between investment in economic activity and financial
investments. We can add to this the usual financial management of the company:
problems with customer credits, loans, cash flow, etc.
• management control because it is useful for each company to question the relative
contribution of each of its products and more generally on the usefulness of each of
its decisions (to determine which, in the end, are profitable, and which are not)
• accounting, since the reporting of results is mainly done in the classic form of
traditional accounting entries (although simplified)
• the management of human resources at the practical level of the decisions to be
taken in small groups and the necessary conflicts to be resolved, the positions of the
various parties being sometimes significantly different

• computer science because nothing prevents participants from trying to emulate the

• marketing in terms of decisions to be made about products and range (price,

1.4 - Simulation basics
On a practical level, the simulation consists of the following elements:
• A market. To set the ideas, we can consider that this is the market for moisturizers
in Switzerland in 2040, but this hypothesis is not really essential for the rest of the
game
• Two axes structuring this market.
• Companies, banks and a stock exchange.
The participants are, depending on their number, divided into:
• 4 to 10 companies (ideally: 3 to 5 participants per company)
• 1 to 4 banks (ideally: 2 to 4 bankers per bank)
• The stock market, on the other hand, works automatically.
The companies are all in the same situation at the beginning of the simulation. They do
not yet have any activity but have capital that will allow them to immediately launch

very precisely by the statement of equity (items "capital", "reserves" and "carry-forward")
on the liabilities side of the balance sheet. At the end of the simulation, the ranking of
companies and banks is based on this single criterion, which must be considered as the
common goal of all, to the exclusion of all others. As for the means to be used to achieve
this goal, everyone is free to choose: safety or risk-taking, volume strategy or high-end
strategy: everything is allowed, everything is organized in the simulation setting so that
different paths can be taken. It's up to you to find them.
2 - Decisions
2.1 - Investments and Fixed Assets
The level of tangible fixed assets (which are always referred to simply as "fixed assets" in
the simulation, intangible assets are never named as such) directly determines the
production capacity of companies according to the following cardinal rule:
6

--- Page 7 ---
Total production capacity in period n = Amount of fixed assets at the start of period n /
100
Compliance with this equation is underpinned by the fact that depreciation is supposed
to accurately reflect the obsolescence of the production tool, and consequently its
reduction in capacity. The amount of fixed assets is therefore always adjusted to the
quantity of products they produce.
This production capacity must be understood for all products combined, as fixed assets
are never considered to be dedicated to a specific product. We can notice, as was already
the case above for monetary units, that the production units are not specified: they don't
really matter, but if we take the example of the moisturizing cream market, we can
assume that they are batches of 1000 boxes of 20 kilos.
The type of depreciation used in the simulation is a bit special: it is a specific form of
declining balance system, in which the depreciation rate, of 25% per year, acts on the
total amount of residual net fixed assets. The following three equations are therefore
always true:
Amount of fixed assets at the start of period n = Amount of fixed assets from last
balance sheet + investment of period n
Depreciation period n = 0.25 x Amount of fixed assets at the beginning of period n
Amount of fixed assets at the end of period n = 0.75 x Amount of fixed assets at the
beginning of period n
Investments in production, for their part, are free throughout the simulation, but they
cannot take on a negative value (which would correspond, in spirit, to a sale of fixed
assets).
While this set of rules may seem restrictive at first, it quickly proves to be very easy to
apply, and to have a certain accounting realism.
Let's take an example: each company starts the simulation with a zero fixed asset amount.
Its production capacity therefore depends only on the investment it decides to set for
that period. If it wants to produce 200, it has to invest 20000. We will then have:
Fixed asset end of period 0 = 0
Production investment at the beginning of period 1 = 20000
Fixed asset start of period 1 = Fixed asset end of period 0 + Investment in production start
of period 1 = 0 + 20000 = 20000
Production capacity period 1 = Fixed asset beginning of period 1 / 100 = 20000 / 100 =
200
Depreciation period 1 = 0.25 x 20000 = 5000
7

--- Page 8 ---
Fixed asset end of period 1 = 0.75 x 20000 = 15000
Comments: at the beginning, fixed assets (which should be thought of as physical
production tools: machine tools, computer equipment, etc.) correspond exactly to the
investment in production. Production capacity is therefore obtained by dividing one or
the other by 100. On the other hand, at the end of the year, fixed assets have aged. They
no longer allow us to produce as much for the following year. The decrease in capacity is
directly proportional to the book value of depreciation, i.e. 25%. This leads to a situation
where fixed assets are only worth 15000 at the end of the period, which corresponds to
a residual production capacity of 150.
In other words, if the company does not reinvest in period 2, it will only be able to produce
150. If it wants to produce more (e.g. 240), it must reinvest an amount corresponding to
the additional capacity it wants to obtain (here a capacity of 90, i.e. a production
investment of 9000). Indeed, we will then have:
Fixed asset start of period 2 = Fixed asset end of period 1 + Investment in production
beginning of period 2 = 15000 + 9000 = 24000
Production capacity period 2 = Fixed asset start of period 2 / 100 = 24000 / 100 = 240
Depreciation period 2 = 0.25 x 24000 = 6000
Fixed asset end of period 2 = 0.75 x 24000 = 18000
And so on...
Be careful : it is important to always invest in sufficient quantities to ensure the desired
production. In the event of insufficient demand for production investment, a partial
automatic adjustment (50%) is calculated by the computer program, but the level of
production remains below the desired level, and stock shortages are to be feared. On the
other hand, in the event of too much investment in production, the risk is that of
overcapacity, which is paid for by depreciation disproportionate to sales (here too, an
automatic adjustment is made by the program, but only for half of the error).
In addition, the requested production investment is only granted if the cash position
allows it. In the event that the cash flow (after the stock market movements at the
beginning of the financial year) does not cover all the production investment requested,
the surplus part is only half executed. This automatic correction of the program has the
function of securing the accounts of imprudent companies by prohibiting them from
overdrawing too large, but in return it limits their ability to produce, and consequently to
make a profit.
It is always necessary to remember to check the adequacy between the production
objectives and the amount of fixed assets. On the one hand, they naturally tend to
exhaust themselves "on their own", without us noticing, by the sole play of depreciation
(25% being a high rate). On the other hand, the ease with which one can decide to invest

--- Page 9 ---
risks making people forget that investments have a cost (in this case, the depreciation
involved) that must be made profitable on pain of poor results.
2.2 - Production
The production of products is freely decided under the conditions indicated in the
previous section. Fixed assets are freely reallocated to any of the products in the portfolio
from one period to the next.
The unit cost of production (assumed to represent the cost of raw materials consumed
and labour allocated to production) is largely degressive according to the quantities
produced (all products combined) in accordance with the principle of economies of scale.
This cost before the allocation of the quality budget (see below) is the same for all the
products of the same company, and at most equal to 50 per unit produced. If it cannot be
known exactly a priori, the experience of the first periods helps to make an increasingly
plausible evaluation as the simulation progresses, insofar as it is recalled each time on the
results sheets. At the beginning of the simulation, it will be wise to count on a cost close
to the maximum.
The actual value of the production unit cost is shown in the profit and loss sheets, before
and after the quality budget is allocated to the cost accounts. The overall production
expenses appearing in the general income statement take into account both the cost of
production and the quality budget.
2.3 - Loans and cash management
The investment can be financed by any means, starting of course with the company's
equity. It is also possible, to cover the investment or for any other reason, to resort to
borrowing.
This borrowing can take two forms:
• A loan negotiated with the bank(s) present in the simulation. Its rate and amount
can be freely discussed and set between any bank and any company that finds it in
their interest.
• A usurious loan. It is systematically granted, regardless of its amount, to any
company that requests it, but its rate is a major obstacle to systematic use. This rate
is set at 16% (non-negotiable).
Please note : these loans, whatever their form, can be renegotiated each year. They are
not renewed by default. It is up to the teams to define their financial needs and borrow
accordingly during each period of play. It is a common mistake among beginner teams to
neglect this aspect, and to "forget" to renegotiate their loan(s) in period 2 or 3. The
consequence is either a strongly negative cash flow (the liabilities are no longer sufficient
to balance the assets once the bank loans have disappeared) and agios that largely
penalize the accounting result in the following year, or an inability to invest in order to
produce (see above).
The loans run from January 2 to January 2. The balance sheets, which are published on
the first of January, still take this into account even if they are running out. We must
anticipate this imminent disappearance in the financial decisions to be taken for the
coming period.
9

--- Page 10 ---
In the event of positive cash flow after the movements at the beginning of the financial
year (which include the variation in borrowing, investment in production and stock
market movements), the balance gives rise to the payment of financial income at the rate
of 5%. If there is a cash deficit, it causes significant interest (20%). Cash flow difficulties
are not considered a sign of insolvency but a sign of lack of foresight. A chronically
negative cash flow does not therefore give rise to sanctions other than those, already
severe it is true, inflicted by the very high rate of agios.
The month-to-month cash position is never taken into account in the simulation. The
participants' only concern should be to break even at the beginning of the exercise,
because it is on this indicator alone that the consequences are exerted in the context of
the simulation.

2.5 - Differentiation
From this presentation, one can immediately get the impression that the most
advantageous positioning is the one that places the product at the heart of the most
important segment. It's true... as long as other companies do not make the same
calculation, because simulation is governed by a more powerful principle than that of
positioning alone: the principle of differentiation. Indeed, a product only sells well if it is
unrivalled. However, the more attractive a segment is, the more likely it is that it will be
crowded.

For the sake of completeness, it should be added that the annoyance still depends on the
level of attractiveness of the product considered to be annoying. It makes sense that an
attractive product would gain more customers over its rivals than a mediocre product.
The discomfort exists regardless of the product considered. That is to say, two products
of the same company can get in each other's way in the same way as two products of
competing companies. To avoid this risk of cannibalization, it is important to ensure that
you have a portfolio of products that are more complementary than rival.
Because of the existence of these differentiation problems, we find ourselves in a
dilemma inspired by game theory in relation to competing companies: either everyone
blindly follows their interest... and this harms everyone, everyone converging in the same
places, or... Companies are trying to find a more appropriate solution.
What can this solution be? The "sharing of the cake" of course, i.e. the agreement
between the different actors of the simulation to position the products in relation to each
other without causing each other too much harm. It should be noted here that the
simulation intentionally absent any legal authority enforcing any anti-cartel law. The
option of absolute free competition (and not of organized free competition, as most
states try to organize in real economic life) was deliberately chosen here to allow the
14

--- Page 15 ---
participants to experiment without hesitation with the advantages and risks of alliance
strategies.
Who is responsible for organising a distribution of market areas? To no one in particular.
There is no supervisory authority in the simulation that can play this role. On the other
hand, any discussion between two, three or more companies is possible. Everyone will be
able to make commitments to others and demand that they do the same. But all this
construction will have to be part of a strategy of self-organized alliance.
Commitments will be all the more difficult to keep as they will have to be made in an
uncertain environment. As the positioning is marred by a certain inaccuracy, one can
always argue that the hazard is to explain to one's partners/opponents a positioning that
is more embarrassing (for them) than expected. But why would it be in our interest to
betray our commitments? Simply because the effect of differentiation is such that if it is
more advantageous for everyone to move away from the center of a segment, it is also
more advantageous, once the center of this segment has been cleared, to place oneself
back there!

2.6 - Product launches and discontinuations
Throughout the simulation, companies can manage a variable number of products
knowing that:
• A maximum of two new products can be launched per game period
• A maximum of two old products can be dropped per game period
• The maximum range, for a given period of play, is 4 different products
• It is possible, within the same period, to discontinue a product and immediately
relaunch a new product with the same number

2.7 - Price and build quality
The manufacturing qualities and selling prices are independent for each of the company's
products. It is considered possible (as is common in the detergent, hygiene or grocery
markets) for the same company to manage products bearing brands that have nothing to
do with the minds of consumers. This does not harm either the brand image of the
products or that of the company, insofar as there is no synergy between the products
apart from the fact that they share the same production tool.
The prices shown are not prices per product, but per unit of production. For this reason,
the acceptable price range in the simulation is often between 100 and 300.
Price is the factor that most influences demand. However, if the price elasticity is very

At the beginning of this curve, there is no effect of advertising investments on demand.
This is understandable for two reasons:
• On the one hand, any advertising action involves fixed costs (in particular creative
costs) which, on their own, do not generate any sales.
• On the other hand, there is a minimum "cost of entry" in the crowded territory of
advertising communication to the general public. To gain a foothold in the consumer's
imagination, to exist in his eyes, you must have repeated his message a minimum of
times. In terms of communication, there is what can be called a threshold effect:
below a certain threshold, the effect is almost nil.
Secondly, advertising is quickly effective, then its effect gradually becomes saturated,
which generally corresponds to the fact that a market is never infinitely expandable, and
in particular to the fact that advertising actions that are too repetitive end up having no
marginal effect on sales (concept of diminishing returns). The first S-curve is more
pronounced than the second, because the threshold effect is clearer than the effect of
diminishing returns.
It's important to note that zero ad budget doesn't mean zero sales. There are products
that sell very well without advertising (and this in all levels of range). Setting an
advertising budget at 0 can be a good calculation in areas of low advertising importance,
or for a product in decline.
What is obviously less desirable is to be positioned either right at the entrance of the S
(you have just paid the entry costs, but you don't get any benefit from it) or too far to the
right of the curve (you could get the same effect by spending less). These two undesirable

2.11 - Stock Exchange Transactions
In the simulation, it is possible to buy shares of your opponents. The capital of each
company and each bank is available on the stock market in the form of 400 freely
exchangeable securities (without voting rights, therefore without the possibility of taking
control). Each of these securities entitles the holder to a corresponding fraction of the
dividends paid in the event of profit (1/400 per share).
In the rest of the simulation, the stock market quotes evolve according to:
• of the results of the listed company. A good result makes the stock go up, a bad
result makes it go down. This result is considered in the simulation both for its
absolute value, and for its value in relation to the capitalized value of the company,
as is generally the case in real economic life through the PER.
• of the more or less strong demand for the stock. High demand drives the stock up,
weak or non-existent demand gradually lowers it. However, the demand taken into
account is reduced to that which can actually be served. It is therefore useless, if you
own all the shares of a company, to continue to ask for them in order to artificially
increase its price.
At each period of play, the new odds are made public. Decisions to buy or sell are executed
on this basis. In the event that the demand for a permit is greater than the total available,
this available total is distributed among the applicants on a pro-rata basis based on the
application.
25

--- Page 26 ---
It is not possible to buy the securities of your own company (or bank). On the other
hand, it is possible to engage in cross-shareholdings with partner companies, in particular
in the context of the creation of alliances on other aspects (market agreements).
Capital gains and losses are only recognised when the shares are resold. They are taxed
at the same rate as all profits. Pending their resale, the shares are valued on the balance
sheet at their purchase price. Thus, even if the capitalized value of the securities in the
portfolio appears for information in the income sheets, it has no book value until these
securities are sold. All securities will nevertheless be valued at their final price during the
final period of play so that all capital gains and losses are integrated into the final result.
In addition, the ownership of securities gives rise to the payment of dividends. The rule is
that any company that makes a profit puts 50% of its net profit in reserve and distributes
50% of it in the form of dividends. These dividends are divided into 400 shares
corresponding to the 400 shares of the company present in the simulation. They are paid
with a staggered annuity to avoid circular, accounting as well as IT references.
3 - Other important factors
3.1 – Customer brand loyalty
The demand for a product does not depend only on the factors mentioned above. Or
rather, it does not depend solely on these factors observed over the period under
consideration.
The application is actually made up of two elements:
• an attraction effect that depends on the factors of the current period (this is the
most important element)
• a loyalty effect (which plays a secondary role) which in turn depends on two factors:
o how these factors may have been effective in the past (hysteresis factor)
o the consistency of the pricing policy from one period to the next (a strong
price variation tends to reduce loyalty while on the contrary a constant price
favours it)
Although the concept of a "cash cow" cannot really be considered valid in the simulation,
it can still be considered that the marketing mix of a product benefits from evolving
according to its position in its life cycle. In particular, greater commercial aggressiveness
at the time of the launch of a given product may make it possible to win customers who,
once loyal, will remain partially captive even in the event of a decrease in attractiveness
(slight increase in price, slight decrease in advertising, etc.). However, such a rule should
be taken with caution. Indeed, if loyalty exists in the simulation, it plays a limited role, and
it is better to keep in mind the image of disloyal consumers always ready to switch brands
rather than loyal consumers who are easy to exploit once conquered.
In any case, if you want to keep your loyal consumers, a price increase will benefit from
being made gradually to avoid inconsistency problems. On the other hand, in the case of
a product that sells poorly from the moment of its launch, the entire mix can be brutally
modified: you only risk losing the loyal ones, and if they are few in number, they should
not be an obstacle to the "relaunch" of the product towards new targets.

--- Page 27 ---
3.2 - Stocks
Unsold units are automatically placed in stock. Their valuation is then based on 50 per
unit. The storage itself results in a cost of $4 per unit in stock per play period. This storage
cost is deducted from the income statement in the following period.
3.3 - Overhead Expenses
Every company generates overhead. Their amount is 2000 plus 1000 per product in the
range. This incremental increase includes increases related to the presence of a product
manager, an additional line in production, etc. For banks, the general costs are set at 4000,
and cover in particular a non-cancellable subscription to the panel.
While these overhead costs are negligible for companies with significant economic
activity, they can be more inconvenient for companies with a small turnover.
3.4 - Economic situation
The entire game world is collectively affected by a business index, expressed as a
percentage in the result sheets. This index should be understood as a multiplier that
applies to the demand for each product. A business index of +2% affects all applications
with a coefficient of 1.02. A business index of -5% with a coefficient of 0.95. Etc.
The economic index mentioned in the results sheets does not refer to the coming period,
but to the past period. However, since the evolution of this index is not surprising and is
limited to a few points per period of play, it is not difficult to predict its probable future
value based on an extrapolation from the past.
In addition, the simulation takes place without inflation.
3.5 – Marketing research
It is possible, at each turn of the game, to order market research. These studies have the
major advantage of helping to better understand the mechanisms of the model, and as
such they are an important help during the first periods of play, but in return they pay off.
The studies are only available to companies, as banks do not have the possibility to order
them outside the panel, to which they are automatically subscribed. The three proposed
studies are as follows:
• The panel (cost: 2000): it allows you to know the details of positioning, price,
quality, and sales for each product of each company, while leaving in the shadows a
certain number of elements that can contribute to the success of a product
(advertising, sales force, credit, loyalty, etc.). The panel is crucial for those who want
to either detect and copy leading products, or on the contrary seek to detect less
congested areas to prepare for an opportunistic launch.
• The study of the mix (cost: 1500): this makes it possible to know, for all the products
of the commissioning company, the level of effectiveness of the various variables
influencing demand. It is assumed that a complete study has been done with
consumers and distributors to determine whether age, price, advertising, positioning,
etc. of the products put on sale were satisfactory or not. In practice, it is the effects
of the variables as calculated by the model that are given to the participants. The
study also provides the elasticities of some of these variables to allow their position
to be refined on the curves provided above. As in any study of this type, the results
are given with a certain margin of uncertainty (plus or minus 3%).
27

--- Page 28 ---
• Simulation (cost: 1000): it makes it possible to know the level of sales that would
be achieved by an imaginary product with a certain positioning, a certain level of
price, quality and advertising (and benefiting from the same sales force and the same
customer credit as all the other products of the company) in the current market
conditions. This simulation is sold by a specialized firm which, despite all its science,
cannot guarantee an accuracy of more than 5% (more or less). One of the advantages
of simulation is that it forecasts future sales over a period of three years, taking into
account the loyalty that would be gradually created. Its flaw lies in its relative
imprecision, aggravated by the fact that between a simulation and a very real launch,
it is enough for a competing product to be launched in the coveted segment for the
gap to be considerable.
3.6 - Income Tax, Subsidies
The company's profits, regardless of their source (operations, capital gains, etc.) are taxed
at a fixed rate of 50%.
In the event of a catastrophic situation for one of the companies in the market or one of
the banks, the facilitator has the possibility of granting subsidies. However, this option is
only to be used as a last resort, since it makes it difficult to accurately evaluate the
company at the end of the game.
In the most frequent case, the host systematically subsidizes 90% of the losses of loss-
making companies during the first period of play in order to avoid too great an initial
imbalance between the different teams. The subsidy rate is then reduced to 60% of the
losses for the rest of the game (so as to prevent some companies from entering a vicious
circle of poor results from which it would be impossible to escape).

Using the Table
It would be illusory to think that the game consists in maximizing all the effects of the
painting. It's easy to do it: just put the products on sale at very low prices, with a colossal
advertising budget and hordes of sellers... The products will sell in droves... and the
company will collapse. In reality, it is not a question of finding a maximum, but an
optimum to achieve satisfactory efficiency at a low cost.
It should not be concluded that, because of the lack of weighting between the different
coefficients, all variables are equally important. This is not the case because the range of
variation of the variables is not the same. Customer credit, for example, is not very
important and it only varies between very narrow limits (it will never be small). On the
other hand, the effects of price or differentiation (important variables) can be very small,
and therefore very penalizing. It is up to the teams to calculate which variables to use to
increase the demand for their products at the lowest cost. In any case, it is possible to
make reasoning such as:
• If the age effect is 80% for a one-year-old product and 95% for a two-year-old
product, all other things being equal, 19% more can be sold in the second year (95%
divided by 80% equals 1.19).
• The effect of advertising is 64% for a given product, and if we allocate an advertising
budget that allows it to reach 96%, we can, all other things being equal, increase sales
by 50% (96% divided by 64% gives 1.5).

The elasticities indicated by the program correspond to their usual academic definition.

demand would increase to 345 (350 minus 1.45%), if all other variables and competition
remained equal elsewhere.
This indication is not very useful as such, but it is possible, without too much risk, to
extrapolate the measure of elasticity (theoretically pointal) to an area a little wider than
1%.
If we assume that the extrapolation to the neighboring 10% area is legitimate, we can
instead say: if the product were sold at 150 (136 plus 10%), the demand would increase
to about 299 (350 minus 14.5%). We can also consider the opposite possibility and
calculate that if the product were sold for 122 (136 minus 10%), we could invest 401 (350
plus 14.5%). It is up to the team concerned to calculate whether such changes are
desirable or not.

horizontal, as is the case for product 2). However, this does not prove that a budget for
2000, for example, would not be profitable, because thanks to it we would exceed this
first threshold and we would gain a very different zone of elasticity. Caution, therefore,
not to extrapolate too far, especially for irregular curves.
5 - Bankers' Game
The natural range of negotiation between bankers and corporate borrowers extends
between 5% (the risk-free rate of return on the banks' cash flow, which therefore has no
interest in lending at less) and 16% (the rate of usurious borrowing, which is easy to obtain
without justification by the borrowing companies, which therefore have no interest in
borrowing more). Between these two limits, companies that need money and bankers
who hold it more or less find what they are looking for. Where exactly between these two
boundaries? This is the whole purpose of the negotiation between the stakeholders.
One might think that the banker is necessarily a winner, and that he does not take any
risks. It's not true. In most cases, the banker is actually paid. But sometimes a company is
a bad partner for a bank. This is the case when two conditions are met:
• The company has the worst result of all the companies on the market
• The company loses at least 1000
If these two conditions are met, the company is considered to be in difficulty. It is then
assumed that its suppliers are wary of it, no longer grant it preferential rates, or even take
it to court at the slightest delay in payment. The banker, who is in solidarity with the
company in order to be able to recover the amount of his loan, is sanctioned as follows:
the bank does not receive the interest on the loan, and in addition he must pay a fine
equivalent to 50% of this interest.
For its part, the company concerned will still pay, in its income statement, the amount
corresponding to this interest. But it will be assumed that this sum is used to cover other
costs related to his crisis situation.
The situation of a company in difficulty is manifested on the income sheets by an index
called "solvency" which is set at 1 in normal times and goes to -1 in the event of
difficulties.
Bankers are free to organise their thinking as they wish. In any case, they will quickly have
to be able to arbitrate between the use of their capital in the form of loans or stock market
investment.
At each gaming period, bankers can lend a sum equal to what they have left in cash plus
the loans on the balance sheet (which are in fact in cash on January 2nd), plus/minus the
purchases/sales of securities that they plan to make (not counting capital gains or losses,
which are considered to be payable during the year). In addition, they can refinance
themselves under the following conditions:
42

--- Page 43 ---
• The refinancing rate varies by tranche: it is 6% for the part between 0 and 10000,
7% for the part between 10001 and 20000, etc. (1% increase per 10000)
• Refinancing is limited to 100000
• Refinancing is an operation carried out over one year: the following year, it must be
indicated again if it is still necessary, otherwise the bank risks experiencing a strongly
negative cash position.
In the event of a negative cash flow at the beginning of the financial year, banks pay
interest rates on the overdraft amount in the same way as companies (at a rate of 20%).
It is clear that bankers can rightly change the terms of their loans based on their
assessment of the economic health of corporate borrowers. If p represents the
probability that the borrowing company will be insolvent at the end of the gambling
period (probability that the bank must try to assess), the expectation of the profitability
of the loan is indeed from the point of view of the lending bank:
Er = t . (1-p) - 0.5 . t. p = t. (1 - 1.5.p)
where Er is the expected profitability and t is the loan rate.
It should be remembered that in the event of insolvency, the lending bank loses the
equivalent of half of the interest it would normally have received. If t0 represents the base
rate at which the bank can safely invest its money (in this case, the rate of return on cash),
we can write that the loan only becomes desirable when Er exceeds t0.
We therefore arrive at the following inequality:

In any case, banks have no "social" role to play in the simulation. Any company in difficulty
that tries to make them feel guilty about this could be opposed to this paragraph. Their
goal, like that of all other companies, is to maximize their profit. While they must
demonstrate pedagogy and diplomacy for this (especially when companies, most of the
time self-sufficient in the first periods, start to call on them in period 3 or 4 for loans that
exceed their financing capacity, and trade-offs are necessary), these qualities should only
be considered as means to their performance, and not as ends in themselves.
If there is any doubt about the financial health of the borrowing companies, banks can
always choose to insure their loans, which guarantees the repayment of interest even in
the event of insolvency. However, this option obviously has a cost (2% of the amount
borrowed), which should be compared to the reduction in risk thus acquired.
6 - Alliances
Bankers can be valuable vectors of information to raise the level of overall simulation
intelligence for the benefit of all. Everyone has something to gain from the market
developing harmoniously and allowing everyone to achieve good results (in particular
thanks to the repercussions of economic activity on stock market prices). In addition to
the fact that the performance of each company is compared with that of other companies
43

--- Page 44 ---
in its game world, it can also be compared to the performance of companies operating in
other game worlds. A shareholder who has to choose to invest more or less in a given
company may give preference to the one that earns 3000 and whose competitors earn
4000, rather than to the one that earns 2000 and whose competitors earn 1000.
In a market where producers "get along" well, a number of coordinated actions may be
part of formal agreements, but in most cases the agreements are invisible, simply the
result of private discussions between actors, and difficult to identify.
The only role of the facilitator from the point of view of agreements between teams is, at
their request, to validate certain contractual agreements and to carry out the terms and
conditions provided for in these agreements. However, an agreement can only be
validated by the facilitator (and therefore take the force of law) if the facilitator considers
its content to be lawful. Broadly speaking, lawful agreements may relate to long-term
commitments between companies and banks (loans planned over several consecutive
periods), or the conditioning of a loan on a certain number of commercial decisions (for
example, a loan could only be granted on condition that it is actually used for an
investment in production). On the other hand, any agreement on price levels, exchanges
of information, or market sharing in terms of positioning, would be considered illegal.
Therefore, any agreement between companies on these points would only be a matter of
the word given, and no sanctions would be exercised in the context of the simulation
against the offenders in the event of non-compliance with the agreement.
Let's imagine, for example, that a company and a bank want to make a commitment, from
period 1, to borrow 10000 over 3 years at 10% repayable in fine. The two contracting
parties may draw up a contract specifying precisely the terms of their agreement
(duration of the agreement, amounts committed), and the penalties/compensation to be
applied in the event of non-compliance (or default, or absence, or error in good faith,
etc.). If the contract is considered formally valid by the facilitator (the conditions for this
validity being in particular that the reference periods and the terms of termination are
clearly indicated), it is registered, and during the following periods, it becomes law; That
is to say, in the event of non-compliance with the contract accompanied by a complaint
by the aggrieved company, the host compensates the latter and penalizes his (ex-)partner
according to the terms and conditions provided for in the contract. It should be noted
that making a commitment of this type does not in any way exempt the need to enter the
information on the successive decision sheets, as the simulation programme is only
connected to the decision sheets and not to the agreements between partners.
If, on the other hand, two companies wish to make a commitment not to harm each other
in a given segment, it is up to them to find their own means of agreement between them,
as the host refuses to endorse this type of alliance, which is considered illegal.
7 - Frequently Asked Questions
Question : What do you need to do to win?
Answer : Start by reading the manual! Almost everything is there.
Question : What is the most important section of the manual?

Answer : After this one, the consecutive sections "demand coefficient", "use of the table",
and "elasticities".
Question : Can luck make you win?
Answer : Yes. It cannot be ruled out that decisions taken in a risky way are rather
fortunate. However, this situation has a low probability of reoccurring throughout the
simulation for the same company.
Question : Can luck make you lose?
Answer : No. It can momentarily diminish a result. But a company that loses a lot of
money has inevitably made mistakes or taken risks (sometimes without realizing it, by the
way).
Question : What are the qualities required to win with a company?
Answer : A mind turned towards play, manipulation, experience rather than an overly
studious, contemplative or passive mind; a sense of proportion more than a sense of
detail; a certain intellectual flexibility to avoid an overly academic treatment of
simulation; the ability to be informed, to establish relationships with other teams;
possibly knowledge of mathematics (analysis); in computer science and programming.
Question : What are the qualities required to win with a bank?
Answer : The ability to process a large amount of commercial and accounting data in a
comparative manner; the ability to obtain information, to exchange with other
participants; diplomacy; availability; resistance to stress (which will have to be faced
during loan negotiations or refusals); prior in-depth knowledge of corporate finance is not
necessary.
Question : What is the point of being a banker?
Answer : The bankers' game provides a broader vision of the simulation than that of a
particular company, the assurance of a higher average result than that of companies and
the possibility of a more in-depth stock market game.
Question : What are the variables that should be of most interest?
Answer : For companies : first of all, the comparative positioning/differentiation of each
of its products; price; then quality and advertising. For the banks : the evolution of the
stock market quotes of the various companies (much more important than an interest
rate point on which the participants nevertheless work hard to negotiate, sometimes for
hours!)
Question : How do you know if a product is profitable?
45

--- Page 46 ---
Answer : By developing complete and accurate cost accounting yourself. The cost
accounting elements given in the simulation are intentionally minimalist and only allow a
rough estimate of the profitability of each product. It is also common to have a range of
products that appear to be all profitable, i.e. with a positive margin, and an overall
negative accounting result. This is linked to the fact that a certain number of expenses,
sometimes of large amounts, are not charged to the analytical accounts.
Question : Is there a synergy between all the products in the range?
Answer : From a production point of view, yes, since the production tool can be used
indifferently to produce any product in the range. It is therefore sometimes advantageous
to limit the production of a product that sells poorly and has a large inventory, and to
reallocate the production capacity thus freed up to a younger or more demanded
product. On the other hand, from a commercial point of view, the only synergy comes
from the joint sales force, but it must in any case see its workforce grow in proportion to
the number of products to be sold in order to maintain constant efficiency. It is especially
important to note that an advertising investment on one product in the range has
absolutely no effect on the other products, contrary to what sometimes happens in reality
(for example in the automotive market, food market, etc.).
Question : When should you advertise a product?
Answer : Not systematically in any case. In general, it is almost essential to advertise on a
generalist product (but not on a specific product). It is also more effective to advertise
when the product is young, which means one or two years. It can be clever to launch a
product without advertising knowing that it will sell poorly, and to wait until the beginning
of its maturity (second year) to push it into advertising, in order to work on a more solid
foundation. A weak product (poorly positioned, too competitive, too old, etc.) generally
does not deserve a heavy advertising investment. On this type of product, it is better to
limit costs.
Question : How much growth in demand can we expect between the year of a product

Answer : 20% to 40% if nothing else changes. We can be convinced of this by looking at
the figures of the study called "simulation" given as an example below the analysis of the
marketing mix.
Question : When should you give up your products due to aging?
Answer : It is up to you to determine it experimentally. Usually after 3 and 8 periods of
play.
Question : Can an old product be replaced by a new product with the same positioning?
Answer : Yes, and it's a classic way.

Question : How many products should be launched in the first half of the game?

benefit from taking an interest in the stock market.
Question : Should we be worried about a slightly negative result in the first two periods

Answer : No, this is a normal phenomenon; companies start from scratch in a non-existent

9 – Common mistakes
Over-quality : quality is added to the unit production cost, it quickly becomes very
expensive. It therefore only makes sense if consumers are willing to pay proportionately
more for the products concerned, which is rarely the case. In the long term, the vast
majority of products will be sold at a quality level between 5 and 30, although
occasionally (especially for the organic segment), the quality can go up to 80 or 100.
Over-communication : each advertising budget constitutes a significant fixed cost
(because of the efficiency threshold to be crossed, between 2000 and 5000 depending
on the segment) which only makes sense if the volume of business on the product
concerned is very high. For products that have exceeded their maturity, limited by the
competition, not very effective for other reasons, or simply do not intrinsically need it
(bottom of the mapping), it is strongly recommended not to do it at all. In the long term,
between 50 and 80% of products could be sold without advertising. On the other hand,
for advertising-sensitive segments (national and green brands), advertising should
generally be maintained for at least two to three periods after launch to avoid triggering
the decline too early.
Average prices : the model encourages a fairly clear-cut pricing policy: around 120, or
around 300. Around 200, we are generally disappointed by the level of sales.
50

--- Page 51 ---
Prices that are too low : given the fixed costs to be shared, it is generally not possible to
generate a profit by selling at less than 100, even for a minimum quality level (0). On
products aimed at very large volumes, the margin can double between 100 and 120 if
the price elasticity is not too high.
Too large a sales force : In many cases, 5 to 10 salespeople are enough. Beyond that, it
is necessary to control the elasticity of demand to this variable. Low elasticity signals a
disappointing marginal return.
Lack of customer credit : You should always do at least some customer credit.
Unbalanced ranges, unrealistic forecasts : some very positive earnings forecasts are
based on a single false assumption (usually a forecast of high volume sales on an
expensive product). The market often reminds the culprits to be more realistic when it
comes to the actual results.
